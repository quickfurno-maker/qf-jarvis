# Riya HUMAN GOLD V1 — review workflow

**Slice:** HGV1-A · **Decision:** [ADR-0108](../decisions/ADR-0108-riya-human-gold-v1-authoring-and-calibration.md) · **Companions:** [authoring rubric](./riya-human-gold-authoring-rubric.md), [wave plan](./riya-human-gold-wave-plan.md), [governance](./riya-intelligence-dataset-governance.md)

Review is the binding constraint on this corpus. Authoring throughput is not — a wave of 72 can be
written faster than it can be honestly read, and a corpus that outruns its reviewers is a corpus
nobody has actually checked.

---

## What the machine checks, so you do not have to

Everything deterministic runs before a human looks at anything, in one pass:

- the full RID-F1 gate — deep re-proof of every state and turn, lineage split isolation, cross-split
  duplicates and near-duplicates, the protected P10 exam firewall, privacy and secret scanning, the
  volatile-business-fact authority rule, risk-based review sufficiency, and the release binding;
- the Gold matrix — split, language, primary kind, persona, risk class, difficulty, starting phase and
  declared provenance must each match the assignment; required secondary kinds must be present;
  required authority fact classes must be both supplied and cited; plus unfulfilled slots, orphan
  trajectories, and depth deviation beyond one turn;
- the repetition report — unique replies, exact repeats, repeated openers and closers.

**Do not spend review attention on any of the above.** If it can be checked mechanically, it has
been. Findings come back named, so an author fixes a specific field rather than re-reading their
brief.

The exception is the rest of `forbiddenPatterns` — false urgency, canned openers, guilt, competitor
attacks. Those are judgement calls with no deterministic owner, and nothing pretends to enforce them.
They are yours.

---

## What only a human can check

Four things, in this order.

### 1. Is it actually human-authored?

**This one is on you, and it cannot be delegated to the tooling.**

The validator checks what the artifact _declares_. It refuses a trajectory whose `source.kind` says
`TEACHER_GENERATED_SYNTHETIC`, and no number of approvals changes that. What it cannot do is tell a
human-written sentence from a model-written one — no deterministic text validator can, and there is
deliberately no AI-authorship detector in this repository. A conversation generated by a model and
then _declared_ human-authored will pass every mechanical gate.

So: human-authorship classification is enforced; physical authorship is process-attested, not
cryptographically inferred. Controlled authoring, provenance bound into the artifact, Git history and
your independent read are what make the claim auditable. There is no proof, and the process is the
only thing standing in that gap.

You are therefore reading for the texture of generated text: the even rhythm, the three-part
sentences, the way every reply acknowledges-then-asks in the same proportion, the enthusiasm that
never varies.

If you believe a trajectory was model-written, say so, and reject it. This is the one finding that is
never a revision request — a model-written conversation cannot be edited into a human-written one.

### 2. Would this be right if the model learned it?

Not "is this a good reply". If the model absorbed this exact move, in this exact situation, would that
be correct behaviour? A reply can be perfectly pleasant and still teach the model to keep selling
after a handoff request, or to answer a question the customer did not ask.

### 3. Does the conversation earn its turns?

Every assistant turn should change something: capture a fact, resolve a concern, move a phase. A turn
that exists to be polite is a turn teaching the model to fill space.

### 4. Does it sound like a person, in this language?

Especially Hindi and Hinglish. Translated English passes every mechanical check and fails the only
test that matters. If it reads like it went through a converter, reject it.

## Risk classes decide how many reviews

| Risk class  | Accepted reviews needed    |
| ----------- | -------------------------- |
| `STANDARD`  | 1                          |
| `HIGH_RISK` | 2, from distinct reviewers |

`HIGH_RISK` is every slot where a wrong answer becomes a commitment: price, discount, payment,
warranty, policy, consent, human handoff, complaint, business action, current availability, identity,
privacy. Roughly a quarter to a third of the corpus.

**Never review your own work.** This is enforced, not requested.

Plan reviewer capacity around the high-risk count rather than the total, because that is what stalls
a wave. The progress board reports `highRiskAwaitingSecondReview` for exactly this reason.

---

## The decision you record

`ACCEPTED` or `REJECTED`, plus the quality dimensions you are satisfied are met. That is the whole
record.

A review carries **no free text and no reviewer name in the progress board**. A notes field becomes a
place where conversation content lives outside the corpus — unvalidated, ungated, and never scanned —
and where one colleague's opinion of another is stored in Git forever. Revision requests go through
whatever the team uses to talk to each other; the dataset records the decision.

## The lifecycle

```
NOT_STARTED → DRAFTING → READY_FOR_REVIEW → ACCEPTED
                 ↑                        ↘
                 └──────────────────────── REJECTED
```

A rejected slot goes back to `DRAFTING` with a bumped revision. The trajectory revision is part of the
record, so a slot that took four attempts says so.

The progress board is workflow metadata only: assignment id, status, trajectory reference, author
reference, review count, last revision. Its summary is deterministic and content-free — counts by
status, by wave, by language, by interaction, plus the high-risk queue.

The board is validated **against the plan**, not on its own. A row saying a high-risk slot is accepted
on one review is internally consistent and globally wrong — and wrong in the direction that hides
itself, since the slot leaves the awaiting-second-review queue and joins the accepted count. The board
validator holds the assignments, refuses that row, and excludes it from the summary, so the headline
number can never report work the same report has just refused.

It also refuses a row for an unknown assignment, two rows for one slot, and a row pointing at some
other trajectory. The corpus review gate remains the authority on the artifact; the board tracks work,
it does not certify it.

## Reviewing a WAVE, not just a slot

At the end of each wave, before it is called done, somebody reads the wave as a whole:

- Read the 72 openers in a row. Do they blur?
- Read the 72 closers in a row. Same question.
- Read the six conversations in one interaction kind, across languages. Are they three languages, or
  one conversation three times?
- Look at the repetition report. Then decide whether the numbers match what you just read — the
  metrics are advisory, and your reading is the calibration.

For Wave 1 this is not optional and not informal: it is the [calibration
gate](./riya-human-gold-wave-plan.md#the-calibration-gate), and waves 2–5 do not start until it
passes.

## What reviewers may and may not change

**May** — after Wave 1, on the evidence of Wave 1:

- the difficulty mix in a cell, if it produced unnatural scenarios;
- a persona assignment that does not fit a situation;
- a depth target that was too shallow or too deep to be real;
- brief wording that two authors read two different ways;
- the repetition threshold, set for the first time against real content.

**May not**, ever:

- the human-authored rule, or the provenance of anything;
- the protected-exam firewall, or the corpus it is pinned to;
- privacy, secret, or business-fact authority gates;
- the split arrangement, or the absence of a holdout;
- the requirement that a reviewer is not the author.

"The gate is inconvenient at this scale" is not a calibration finding. It is the gate working.
